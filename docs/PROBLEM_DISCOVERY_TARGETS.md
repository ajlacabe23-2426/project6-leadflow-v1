# LeadFlow Problem-Discovery Targets

Status: evidence-oriented research queue. These companies are not assumed to need LeadFlow. The purpose is to interview operators whose public workflows suggest meaningful intake, scheduling, diagnosis, field-work, handoff, or multi-step service complexity, then determine whether a real unsolved problem exists.

## Research rule

Do not pitch LeadFlow first. Ask how work arrives, how it gets structured, where information is re-entered, what is forgotten, what requires manual judgment, what software is already used, and what still breaks despite that software.

A company only becomes a qualified pilot candidate after the operator confirms a recurring problem with measurable cost/friction.

## Priority research targets

### 1. Individualized Repair Inc. — tool and small-engine repair
Public evidence: owner Martin Stevenson describes downtime as stressful and expensive. The company invites customers to request information, pickup, quotes, service, or repair through its service workflow. This creates several distinct intake types before the technical work even begins.

Research questions:
- How do you distinguish quote, pickup, repair, and information requests when they arrive?
- What information do customers usually fail to provide before you can act?
- Do jobs ever require a second call/message just to collect model, symptoms, pickup details, or approval?
- Where is job status tracked between intake, diagnosis, estimate approval, parts, repair, and pickup?
- What software handles this today, and what still requires manual work?

### 2. Big Lar's Appliance Repair — locally owned in-home appliance repair
Public evidence: locally owned, in-home repair service. Appliance repair industry discussions repeatedly describe a multi-visit workflow: diagnose, identify/order parts, then return to install. Operators also report that large field-service CRMs can feel excessive for small shops.

Research questions:
- What information do you need before deciding whether/how to schedule a service call?
- How do you track diagnosis -> part order -> customer approval -> return visit?
- How often do customers contact you through more than one channel?
- What causes jobs or callbacks to fall through the cracks?
- What system do you use now and which steps happen outside it?

### 3. J T's Appliance Repair — family-owned appliance repair
Public evidence: family-owned Bloomington repair business with a long local history and in-home diagnostic work. The niche has documented workflow friction around scheduling, customer information, parts, and repeat visits.

Use this target primarily to validate whether the multi-visit/parts handoff problem is real locally or already solved by existing software.

### 4. Big A Automotive LLC — mobile automotive repair
Public evidence: mobile repair operation with an identifiable principal and a field-service model. Mobile businesses combine customer intake, location, vehicle details, symptom triage, travel, parts/tools, scheduling, and field execution.

Research questions:
- What minimum information is needed before you decide whether a mobile job is viable?
- How often do people send incomplete vehicle/symptom/location details?
- Do you manually decide urgency, service area, estimated tools/parts, and routing?
- Where do calls/texts/messages get recorded?
- What information has to be copied from the original conversation into the job record?

### 5. Taka Taka Mobile Fab Co — mobile mechanic / welding / fabrication
Public evidence: small mobile operation spanning mechanic, welding and fabrication work, including short-notice jobs. Different service categories likely require different intake information and job qualification.

Research objective: determine whether mixed-service mobile operators experience an intake-classification and scoping problem that generic scheduling tools do not solve well.

### 6. First Strike Termite & Pest Control — locally owned pest control
Public evidence: locally owned and operated, identifiable president/owner Randy Pruett, residential and commercial work, multiple service frequencies, and commercial bids based on quantity and property square footage. The website still directs users to call/contact for pricing and availability.

Research questions:
- How do you decide which inquiries can be scheduled directly versus need an inspection/bid?
- What details are required for residential, commercial, termite, recurring, and one-off calls?
- How are commercial bid requests handed off and followed up?
- What software do you currently use for routes, recurring service, customer records and leads?
- Where does manual re-entry still happen?

### 7. Goodman's Pool Service — locally owned pool service/supply
Public evidence: locally owned business serving McLean County with water testing, chemical guidance, openings/closings, cover work, equipment replacement and liner replacement. This is seasonal and mixes retail/advice, diagnostic and scheduled field-service workflows.

Research objective: test whether seasonal service businesses struggle more with triage/capacity, customer history, diagnostic information, or scheduling spikes than with generic lead capture.

### 8. Reliable Pools Inc. — local pool installation/service company
Public evidence: founded locally in 2020 by Brian and Kristin Peterson, now offers installation, repair, leak detection, inspections, openings/closings, water analysis and equipment work, with a dedicated service team. Contact flow includes phone, text, email, forms and service scheduling.

This is a useful slightly-more-developed comparison company: if its workflow is already fully handled by software, that evidence helps define where the upper boundary of the target market sits.

### 9. Infinity Inspection Services LLC — family-run home inspection
Public evidence: locally owned/family-run inspection business. Home-inspection industry discussions show recurring friction between report-writing software, scheduling and CRM systems, sometimes causing duplicate data entry.

Research objective: ask specifically whether client/agent/property information has to be entered into more than one system and whether scheduling/reporting/customer follow-up live in separate tools.

### 10. Impressive Inspections Inc. — owner-led home inspection
Public evidence: owner Alan Flicek, local company established in 2014, with online scheduling and same-day reports. Public records show the business uses Spectora, so this is not a candidate to assume is under-digitized.

Use as a control interview: learn what Spectora solves well, what still requires manual work, and whether there is any genuine gap. If the answer is "nothing meaningful," record that and do not pitch.

## Deprioritized examples

Property managers using mature resident/owner portals and platforms should generally be deprioritized unless a specific gap is observed. Local examples publicly show online maintenance queues, resident portals and/or AppFolio-like systems. That means their core intake/maintenance workflow is already digitized and they are less useful for testing the lightweight-operations hypothesis.

## Evidence patterns from operator communities

### Appliance repair
Recent operators report being buried in calls, Facebook messages and email while trying to perform technical work, losing track of jobs and struggling to centralize scheduling/intake without hiring office staff. Other appliance-repair discussions describe generic field-service products as too complex and highlight the awkward multi-visit flow of diagnosis -> parts -> return installation.

### Home inspection
Inspectors report a different problem: inspection/report software and CRM/scheduling tools do not always integrate, creating duplicate work. Current inspection products can solve much of this, so the opportunity must be a verified residual workflow problem rather than another general inspection CRM.

### Small field-service businesses
The research supports a general hypothesis worth testing: owner/operators often perform the revenue-producing work and the administrative intake work simultaneously. The commercial question is whether there is a sufficiently frequent, costly handoff problem that existing lightweight tools do not solve.

This remains a hypothesis until local interviews confirm it.

## Interview method: problem interview, not sales call

### Opening
"I'm researching how small service businesses actually manage incoming jobs while they're doing the work itself. I've built some workflow automation technology, but I'm trying not to assume I know what businesses need. Could I ask you a few questions about how your process works today? I'm not calling to sell you software."

### Ask about the last real job
Avoid hypothetical questions. Say:

"Think about the last new customer/job that came in. How did they contact you, and what happened from that moment until the job was actually scheduled or accepted?"

Then follow the workflow step by step.

### Core questions
1. Where do new requests actually arrive: phone, text, Facebook, email, website, referrals, something else?
2. Who handles them when you're busy doing the actual work?
3. What information must you collect before you can decide what to do?
4. What information is most commonly missing?
5. Where do you put that information after receiving it?
6. Do you ever enter the same customer/job information twice?
7. What part of the process still depends on remembering to do something later?
8. Tell me about the last time a request/job became confusing, delayed, duplicated or forgotten.
9. What software/tools do you use today?
10. What does that software do very well?
11. What do you still do through texts, notes, spreadsheets, inboxes or memory?
12. If one part of this process disappeared tomorrow, which would save you the most time or frustration?
13. Does this problem happen often enough that you would actually change how you work to fix it?
14. If someone solved it, how would you know it was working?

### Do not ask
- "Would you use an AI lead platform?"
- "Would this feature be useful?"
- "Would you pay $X for my app?" before a pain is established.

Those questions invite polite speculation.

## Evidence score after each interview

Score the problem, not the company.

- Frequency: 0 rare, 1 occasional, 2 weekly, 3 daily
- Operational pain: 0 trivial, 1 annoying, 2 meaningful delay/rework, 3 lost work/customer/revenue risk
- Existing solution gap: 0 current tool solves it, 1 configuration issue, 2 workaround required, 3 no adequate solution in current workflow
- Measurability: 0 cannot measure, 1 subjective, 2 time/count measurable, 3 directly tied to jobs/revenue/cost
- Willingness to change: 0 no, 1 curious, 2 willing to test, 3 willing to invest time/money

10+ / 15: investigate pilot
6-9: continue discovery
0-5: do not build for this problem

## Skill bridge for the builder

The objective is not simply to generate startup ideas. Each interview should convert into practical capability:

1. Process mapping — turn an operator's spoken workflow into states, inputs, decisions and handoffs.
2. Requirements engineering — distinguish required behavior from feature requests.
3. Automation design — identify what should be deterministic, automated, AI-assisted or human-reviewed.
4. Integration work — connect forms, email/SMS, calendars, databases and existing vertical software only when demanded by evidence.
5. Data modeling — represent customers, jobs, statuses, events and audit history reliably.
6. Security/privacy — minimize collected data, control access and record actions.
7. Measurement — define baseline, latency, completion and failure metrics.
8. Productization — turn one custom workflow into reusable configuration without turning the product into bespoke consulting.

That is the path from "I have an idea" to "I can diagnose a business process, build an automation, verify it, and package the result."

## Immediate experiment

Interview one operator from three different workflow types before choosing a niche:

- appliance/small-engine repair;
- mobile field service;
- seasonal/specialty service (pool or pest control).

Compare the problem scores. Do not select a niche until at least one repeated problem appears in multiple independent conversations or one niche produces unusually strong evidence.
