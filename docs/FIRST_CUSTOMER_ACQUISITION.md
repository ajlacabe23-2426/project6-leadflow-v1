# LeadFlow — First Customer Acquisition Package

## Objective

The first acquisition cycle is not optimized for scale. Its purpose is to find evidence that a real business has a painful lead-response problem, can define that problem operationally, will participate in a controlled pilot, and may pay if LeadFlow produces measurable value.

Success is learning plus commitment, not raw outreach volume.

## 1. Initial target profile

Prioritize owner-led or small-team service businesses that:

- receive inbound requests for appointments, estimates, consultations, or projects;
- have meaningful value attached to each converted lead;
- receive enough leads for inconsistent handling to become noticeable;
- still depend on manual review, inboxes, forms, spreadsheets, texts, or fragmented tools;
- can describe what makes a lead qualified;
- care about response speed;
- have a reachable owner, sales manager, office manager, or operations lead.

### Strong starting categories

- HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing, landscaping and similar home services
- specialty contractors
- marketing/web/creative agencies
- B2B consultancies and professional service firms
- appointment- or estimate-driven local businesses

These are discovery categories, not proven verticals. Do not specialize LeadFlow until evidence supports it.

## 2. Prospect qualification scorecard

Before outreach, score each prospect 0 or 1 on each criterion:

1. Inbound leads are visible/relevant to the business model.
2. A converted lead has meaningful economic value.
3. The business appears to have multiple intake channels or manual intake.
4. Fast response plausibly matters.
5. Qualification is more complex than simply accepting every inquiry.
6. A decision maker is identifiable/reachable.
7. The workflow could be piloted without replacing the company's entire software stack.

5-7 = high-priority prospect
3-4 = discovery prospect
0-2 = low priority

The score is a prioritization aid, not a claim that the prospect has a problem before speaking with them.

## 3. Outreach objective

Do not try to sell software in the first message.

The desired conversion is:

Prospect -> 15-20 minute discovery conversation

The message should communicate:

- a specific operational problem;
- what is being built;
- that feedback is being sought from real operators;
- low commitment;
- no unsupported ROI promises.

## 4. Warm outreach template

Hi [Name] — I’m building a lead-response system for small service businesses and I’m validating it with people who actually manage incoming opportunities.

It’s designed around a simple problem: a business can spend money generating leads, then lose opportunities because intake, qualification, prioritization, or follow-up is inconsistent.

I’m not trying to sell you software on this call. I’d like to understand how your team currently handles a new lead from the moment it arrives until someone follows up or schedules the next step.

Would you be open to a 15-20 minute conversation? If there’s a genuine fit, I may also be looking for a business to help shape a controlled pilot.

## 5. Cold outreach template

Hi [Name] — quick question about how [Business] handles new inquiries.

I’m developing LeadFlow, a system that helps service businesses capture, qualify, prioritize, and consistently follow up with inbound leads. Before expanding the product, I’m interviewing operators to understand where leads actually get delayed or lost in real businesses.

Would you be willing to spend 15 minutes walking me through how a new lead is handled today? This is a research conversation, not a software sales demo.

## 6. Referral request

I’m looking to speak with owners or managers at service businesses that receive regular inbound leads and still have people manually reviewing, qualifying, or following up with them. I’m validating a lead-response system before building deeper integrations. If anyone comes to mind, would you be comfortable introducing us?

## 7. Discovery call structure

Target: 15-25 minutes.

### Opening — 2 minutes

Explain that the goal is to understand the existing process, not persuade them that they need LeadFlow.

### Current workflow — 5 minutes

Ask:

- Where do leads come from?
- Who sees them first?
- Where are they recorded?
- What happens from arrival through first response?
- What systems/tools are involved?

### Qualification and failure points — 7 minutes

Ask:

- What makes a lead worth pursuing?
- What information is commonly missing?
- What causes delayed responses?
- What happens after hours or during busy periods?
- Have good opportunities ever been lost because nobody followed up quickly enough?
- How do you know whether every lead received appropriate follow-up?

Ask for examples rather than hypothetical opinions.

### Economics — 4 minutes

Ask ranges where exact numbers are sensitive:

- Roughly how many leads arrive in a week/month?
- Roughly what is an average converted opportunity worth?
- How much staff time goes into triage/follow-up?
- Which leads are most expensive to miss?

### Existing solutions — 3 minutes

Ask:

- What have you already tried?
- What works well today?
- What frustrates you about the current process?
- What system cannot be disrupted or replaced?

### Pilot signal — 3 minutes

If a real pain is present:

"If I configured a controlled version around your actual qualification and follow-up process, would you be interested in testing whether it improves response consistency?"

Do not force the pilot if the interview does not reveal a meaningful problem.

## 8. Evidence capture sheet

After every interview record:

- company/category;
- interviewee role;
- approximate lead volume;
- intake channels;
- current tools;
- current response process;
- qualification criteria;
- biggest failure point;
- consequence of that failure;
- current workaround;
- average opportunity value/range if shared;
- measurable baseline available? yes/no;
- pilot interest: no / maybe / yes;
- willingness to share sample/anonymized cases: no / maybe / yes;
- willingness to spend implementation time: no / maybe / yes;
- willingness to pay if successful: unknown / weak / moderate / strong;
- requested integrations/features;
- direct quotes/observations (with permission/appropriate handling);
- next action.

## 9. Signal classification

### Weak signals

- "Cool idea."
- "AI is interesting."
- feature suggestions from someone who would not use/buy it;
- social-media likes;
- hypothetical willingness to pay.

### Medium signals

- describes a recurring painful workflow;
- shares current process and numbers;
- schedules a second conversation;
- offers sample/anonymized cases;
- introduces the actual decision maker.

### Strong signals

- commits staff time to configure/test a pilot;
- agrees to measurable success criteria;
- provides representative workflow data under appropriate authorization;
- asks when they can use it;
- agrees to pay for a pilot or continued use;
- signs/accepts an appropriate pilot scope when formalization is needed.

## 10. Design-partner selection

Choose the first design partner based on learning quality, not prestige.

Prefer a business with:

- a clearly observed problem;
- sufficient lead volume;
- simple enough workflow for a first pilot;
- reachable decision maker;
- willingness to collaborate;
- measurable baseline;
- low integration complexity;
- realistic potential economic value.

Avoid making the first pilot depend on several complex production integrations at once.

## 11. Pilot offer

Position the first pilot as a workflow-improvement experiment.

### Included

- map existing intake/qualification workflow;
- configure required fields and scoring/routing rules;
- configure priority/escalation rules;
- generate follow-up drafts;
- provide operator queue;
- retain auditable decision/action history;
- establish baseline and pilot metrics;
- review results with the operator.

### Not currently included as production promises

- autonomous outbound messaging;
- production calendar booking;
- production CRM synchronization;
- replacement of the customer's CRM;
- guaranteed lead or revenue increase.

Any live integration requires a separately verified production-readiness decision.

## 12. Pilot success criteria

Pick 2-4 customer-specific measures before starting. Examples:

- reduce median first-response time;
- increase percentage of leads handled within the agreed SLA;
- reduce unhandled/missed inquiries;
- improve completeness before human follow-up;
- ensure high-priority leads are surfaced consistently;
- reduce manual triage time;
- improve follow-up completion.

Do not change success criteria after seeing the result merely to make the pilot look successful.

## 13. Pricing discovery

Do not ask only, "Would you pay for this?"

After demonstrating value, ask questions such as:

- What does this problem currently cost in staff time or missed opportunities?
- If this reliably solved that problem, where would it rank among your operating expenses?
- Would you expect to pay as software, as a managed service, or as an implementation plus monthly service?
- What would make the price obviously too high?
- What price would make you question whether the solution was capable enough?

Treat answers as evidence, not commitments.

## 14. Initial pricing framework to test

Do not publish this as final pricing yet.

Test three commercial structures in interviews/pilots:

### Design-partner pilot

Free or low-cost for a narrowly scoped first partner when the learning/data access is unusually valuable. Require participation and agreed success criteria in exchange.

### Implementation + recurring service

One-time setup/configuration fee plus monthly hosting/support/integration fee.

### Managed lead-response workflow

Higher recurring price where configuration, monitoring, reporting, and operational support are included.

Final dollar amounts should follow evidence about implementation hours, infrastructure/API costs, support burden, lead volume, customer value, and willingness to pay.

## 15. First acquisition funnel

Initial target:

- identify 20 qualified prospects;
- prioritize 10 for personalized outreach;
- obtain 3-5 discovery conversations;
- identify 1-2 credible pilot candidates;
- select one first design partner;
- define baseline + success criteria;
- run the controlled pilot;
- ask for paid continuation only after value can be evaluated.

These are operating targets, not assumed conversion rates.

## 16. Stop / pivot rules

After 5 serious interviews, review evidence.

Continue with the current segment if multiple operators independently describe the same meaningful problem and at least one shows strong pilot behavior.

Refine the problem/offer if pain exists but the proposed workflow does not match how businesses actually operate.

Test another segment if operators consistently report that existing tools solve the problem adequately or the pain is too minor to justify change.

Do not interpret rejection as a request to add random features.

## 17. What to learn from this phase

The central lesson is that product development is uncertainty reduction.

The code has reduced technical uncertainty: LeadFlow can capture, validate, score, route, draft, persist, and surface leads.

Customer acquisition now reduces different uncertainties:

- Is the problem real?
- Who experiences it most strongly?
- How are they solving it now?
- What does failure cost them?
- Which integration actually matters first?
- What outcome would make the system valuable?
- Will someone commit time, data, reputation, or money?

A founder should not confuse feature completion with business validation.

The first customer is especially valuable because their workflow converts assumptions into requirements. Their behavior is more useful than compliments. A request backed by a real workflow, measurable pain, and willingness to participate should generally outrank speculative feature ideas.

The goal is therefore not "convince someone to buy LeadFlow." The goal is to find the smallest market where LeadFlow solves a measurable problem well enough that payment becomes a rational consequence of value.
